CoffeeFinder

or Creating a CoffeeIndex for Germany

Be it from dubious instagram info posts or from actual academic discussion, most of us are familiar with the so called BigMac Index.
Introduced by The Economist in 1986, it compares the prices of BigMacs converted to US dollars in different countries. As McDonalds is operating in numerous countries around the globe with a fairly consistent menu, especially for brand staples like the BigMac, it servers as a convinient point of comparison. The difference in BigMac prices is meant to highlight differences in purchasing power parity (ppp) of the countries currencies.
Purchasing power parity is a metric to determine the valuation of currencies compared to one another in exchange rates.
The considered prices are those in the capital city of their respective country. For this the data is sourced from McDonalds official website as well as some delivery apps. This results in a fairly straightforward data acquisition process.
Where a simple median wage converted to US dollars often tells little about how that relates to cost of living, the BigMac as a single, widely ditributed and consumed product serves as a feasible proxy for ppp.
It is impacted in accuracy by the fact that it is based on a single companies (or it's franchisees) marketing decisions. A particular supply chain difficulty or marketing push may sway the result one way or the other.
Other approaches to the idea of a price index have similarily looked at singular branded items like a 250ml can of redbull or the iconic IKEA Billy shelf.

What if, instead we used another seemingly universal culinary item, the humble Coffee? Can we create a comprehensive and representative overview of coffee pricing across different regions?
And instead of focusing on the ppp of different countries we use it as a proxy for cost of living in different german regions?
While the single coffee may vary in size and quality, across many different coffeeshops we might have a meaningful proxy for cost of living at our hand.

RQ1: Can a representative CoffeeIndex be created based on publically available information?

After all, coffee prices at the thousands of coffees across germany are up to the discretion of the respective coffee shop owners. And, more crucially, how easily accessible do they make this information? Can we find a centralized source for that information or do we need to go to the coffeeshops web presences themselves. And then, from the orderly PDF with the charme of an excel sheet linked on the home page, with clearly associated items and prices, to a grainy image of the trusty old chalkboard with a few prices scribbled on it - how easy is it to extract these coffee prices?
Inbetween there all manners of fun, weird and sometimes absurd choices people make to display prices on their websites. If they display their prices at all online. Or even more, if they even have an online presence.

Tired from wrangling that jungle of web design, we might feel inspired to persue our side goal of:

Side Goal: Where can we find the cheapest coffee in a city of our choosing?

Out on a walk but short on cash? Where is the next affordable coffee?
Or are you even more hooked to that coffeine and planning long term? What city offers the cheapest coffee?

With renewed energy we may turn to the next step of our investigation:

RQ2: How reliable is the CoffeeIndex as a proxy for economic metrics?

Such an Index could provide an intuitive gage for cost of living relative to median wage. Where the median citizen from Munich may have 35,467€ in expendable income compared to the 23,952 € of the median Berliner, what does that actually mean in everyday life for them?
For example, how many coffees are in the monthly wage of the ordinary citizen of Berlin compared to one from Munich? Such a metric would be less easily swayed by a single corporations business decisions and proxy the cost of living via the joint business decision of dozens of coffee shop owners.
Is there a significant correlation between the coffee prices and economic metrics meassuring income? Can we aggregate enough coffee prices per city to distinguish the dwindling post boom town from the affluent technological hubs?

Data Sources

Café Database Data

For finding the coffee prices of the cafés, a comprehensive data source is needed. Our first idea was to use Google Maps. Google Maps is one of the most widely used map databases by usage and heavily incorporates user-submitted data for points of interest. These points of interest includes places, like stores, restaurants, and, for our use case, cafés. Google Maps stores additional information for many of these places, such as opening times, websites, reviews, and pictures of menus. This makes it an interesting data source. However, for our project, Google Maps was not usable because of ethical and legal concerns, since the Google Maps Terms of Service do forbid any usage for scraping, API usage, and web scraping. As a solution, we decided on OpenStreetMap as an alternative. Similary to Google Maps, OpenStreetMap is a map database that also stores information to many points of interest, such as cafés. However, OpenStreetMap is free and open source and is mostly built by voluntary users, differently to Google Maps, which is a commercial closed source product by Alphabet. Additionally, the OpenStreetMap Project already provides an API for data access of the Map Database called Overpass API. Unfortunately, menu pictures are not available in OpenStreetMap. However, websites are often linked for the cafés. As a result, our main idea for sourcing the coffee price data is to get the websites for the cafés in OpenStreetMap, then scrape the websites for the menus, and at the end, extract the prices from the menus.

Economic Data

Our Economic data is sourced from the german federal and regional statistical bureaus.
Via their Regional Atlas portal they provide access to a variety of different data sets sourced from the different regional statistical agencies.
The demographic data is offered down to a municipal level through their OpenData portal as CSVs. Municipalities are uniquely identified by their bureaucratic regional keys used for accurately mapping them between data sets. For the large cities (above 100.000 Inhabitants) these municipalities correlate well to the actual cities they are named after and thus the once used for querying the coffee shops.
The list of cities above 100.00 Inhabitants was sourced from the Data of the 2022 Mikrozensus yielding 80 such cities. We will look in particular at the following metrics:

  • Disposable Income per capita: Expressed in Euros this metric is a good representation of the money available monthly to people of that city after taxes. It does not consider factors such as rent which we considered to be part of the cost of living overall we want to proxy via the coffee prices. The latest available statistic is from 2023.
  • Tourism in monthly overnight stays per 100.000 inhabitants: This metric is an indicator of a regions touristic appeal. It is based on hotels and hostels of nine or more beds. While this excludes stays a relatives, friends or single day trips it serves as a good predictor of overall tourism. Though it can not distinguish between touristic trips and stays related to business or other non tourism purposes. The used statistic is from 2024.
For both of these data sets little further processing was needed as they are complete for our examined cities and can simply be truncated to exclude the smaller municipalities. The data is already in a uniform integer/float format for further usage.

Methodology

Our architecture for the CoffeeFinder project pipeline primarly consists of three substages as outlined in the CoffeeFinder architecture diagram. Inititally, we retrieve our coffee shops from the overpass API. After the parsing the retrieved cofeeshops into a list of our internal Coffeeshop model holding all coffeshops of a certain city, we run an iteration on each of the coffeshops with provided website URL. Each iteration round of a fixed coffeeshop, can be divided into 2 subcomponents: webscraper and price extractor. The webscraper uses the given website URL of a coffeeshop to collect a set of possible menu URLs. The collection of potential menu URLs is then given to the price extractor which handles different file formats and tries to extract the Cappucino price.

Overpass API

get_coffee_shops_in_city() does load the initial data of each coffeeshop as a GeoDataFrame with OSM tags as columns and normalizes it to use the internal Coffeeshop model instead, directly enforcing a structured data format which persist during the entire pipeline execution. So after the first stage, we hold a list of Coffeeshop Models for each coffeeshop, which initialized the fields served by the overpassAPI and leaves the webscraper and price extractor fields undefined for later addition.

Webscraper

Each coffeeshop that directly provides the menu URL from overpass API is directly forwarded to the price extraction. Otherwise, for each coffeeshop with a given website URL, the webscraper opens the website and searches for links that look like menu pages.
For opening the pages we use Playwright, which is a browser automation library. This allows the webscraper to load websites like a normal browser, so links that are created or changed by JavaScript will also be found. It checks the visible link text and the link URL for words like menu, food, drinks, Speisekarte or Getränke. If no menu link is found on the first page, the webscraper follows internal links of the same website and repeats the search with a limited depth of up to 3 levels and a maximum of 20 page requests. Each page navigation also has a 15 second timeout, so slow or unresponsive pages are skipped instead of blocking the whole pipeline. These limits keep the crawl bounded and prevent the scraper from spending too much time on large or deeply nested websites. All possible menu URLs are collected as candidates and then tested one after another. During this testing, the current candidate is stored in the Coffeeshop model and forwarded to the extractor, which tries to read the menu content and extract the Cappuccino price.

Price Extractor / Analyzer

The price extraction is called by the webscraper after a possible menu URL was found and stored as the current menu_url candidate in the Coffeeshop model. The webscraper requests this candidate URL, reads the returned Content-Type, and forwards the downloaded data to the Analyzer, which selects the matching extraction strategy.
Currently supported formats are image files, PDF documents, text/html pages and application/xhtml+xml pages, so menus can be processed when they are published as normal webpages, downloadable PDFs or images.
For image menus we use pytesseract as the OCR library, which reads the image with Pillow and converts the visible menu content into text using German and English language settings. For PDF menus we use pdfplumber to extract the page text directly, while HTML and XHTML menus are decoded as text and then handled by the same price parsing logic. After a text representation was created, the extractor searches the lines for the Cappuccino keyword and a nearby price pattern, also supporting the case where the name and price are split over following lines.
If the extraction is succesful, the found menu item, price, currency and extraction timestamp are written into the Coffeeshop model, and the code flow is returning back to the coffeeshop iteration logic to continue with the next coffeshop.

Coffeeshop Model

All information extracted during the pipeline execution of each stage enriches our internal Coffeeshop model in place. Keeping this Coffeeshop model as the central data state in our pipeline, allows us to enforce working on structured data during each step, allowing to keep the interfaces of each of the subcomponents well-defined, but independent of each other during development.

At the end of the pipeline, we result in a final list of all Coffeeshop Models of the queried city with the added, extracted information for each of the coffeeshops. Finally, the list of Coffeeshop models is converted into a JSON structure with model_dump(mode="json") and then written as JSON store of the queried city, allowing for a JSON-based frontend design. Because the Coffeeshop model is based on Pydantic, this conversion can also be done at any earlier point in the pipeline whenever an intermediate JSON representation is needed.

Data Cleaning

Our initial data consisted of prices extracted from different formats:
HTML, PDFs and images. These data were inconsistently structured, making the automated extraction of the prices very challenging. In order to make our final data correct and trustworthy, we implemented some data cleaning procedures.

One of the challenges inclusded cafés mistyping Cappuccino. This resulted in data loss. To address this issue we adjusted our regex to also extract from menus that mistyped the word.

Another challenge was the detection of prices in a menu as those varied from menu to menu. Some included Euro signs, some used dots instead of commas. Therefore we had to adjust our script to be able to catch all of these variations.

Lastly we had to make sure that the prices extracted were within a certain boundary for a cappuccino price. To achieve this we set the threshold between 2 and 7 Euros. If a price would exceed or be below that value, the price would be ignored.

Ethics

Coffee Price Extraction

As we already discussed in our Data Sources section, in order to design the website and menu scraping ethically, we considered a few things. First, we pay attention to the robots.txt file of the café websites and only scrape the website if the website owner allows it in their robots.txt. Furthermore, we designed our web scraper in such a way that it limits the access to a single website with a hard limit in the scraping logic. Due to the speed of the entire pipeline, especially due to the time it takes for the whole website to load and to search for the menu, there is more than enough time between accesses to prevent accidentally executing a denial-of-service attack.

Café Database Data

Using OpenStreetMap as a source of café data, instead of Google Maps, which would bring legal and ethical concerns, simplifies our ethical consideration. As OpenStreetMap is an open data project and is licensed under the Open Data Commons Open Database License, its data is already public and free to use for the scope of this project.

Municipalities Economic Data

The used economic data is publically available information readily available through reports, download options and maps. The used data is at a municipal level of the 80 german cities with more than 100.000 inhabitants, with no ties to individual people. Thus at worst, for this subset of our data, we have to consider the impact on these municipalities.
Our use of the data has neither the relevance nor implications to harm the reputation of these municipalities and cities in any meaningful way. We appropriately hedge the accuracy of our results to not overstate their meaning.
